## Grammar, Usage & Pronunciation

### Grammar

#### 1. Third-person singular verbs

**Original**

> “it save it saves my time”

**Corrected**

“It saves me time.”

**Rule:** With third-person singular subjects such as *it*, use the verb ending **-s** in the simple present.

**Drills**
- “The agent checks traffic every five minutes.”
- “The toll road saves us about ten minutes.”

#### 2. “There are” + plural noun

**Original**

> “the food option here is uh so so many kind of foods”

**Corrected**

“There are so many kinds of food here.”

**Rule:** Use **there are** with plural countable nouns.

**Drills**
- “There are a lot of Thai restaurants in Houston.”
- “There are several fried chicken places near me.”

#### 3. Subject form in coordinated phrases

**Original**

> “me and my and my girlfriend have watched this film”

**Corrected**

“My girlfriend and I watched this movie...”

**Rule:** Use the subject pronoun **I** when the phrase is the subject of the sentence.

**Drills**
- “My girlfriend and I are seeing the movie this weekend.”
- “My coworker and I usually take the non-toll route.”

### Usage

#### 1. “quite usual” → “pretty normal”

**Original**

> “it's quite usual”

**Natural**

“It's pretty normal.” / “Pretty normal.”

For an ordinary day, “pretty normal” sounds much more conversational.

#### 2. “search on Google Map” → “check Google Maps”

**Original**

> “I don't have to search on Google Map”

**Natural**

“I don't have to check Google Maps myself.”

For traffic or directions, **check Google Maps** is the natural collocation.

#### 3. “only myself on the car” → “just me in the car”

**Original**

> “only myself on the car”

**Natural**

“It's just me in the car.”

Use **in the car**, not *on the car*, when someone is riding inside it.

#### 4. “speaking to fried chicken” → “speaking of fried chicken”

**Original**

> “So speaking to fried chicken”

**Natural**

“Speaking of fried chicken...”

**Speaking of X** is the fixed expression for connecting to a related topic.

#### 5. “I'm so starving” → “I'm starving”

**Original**

> “I'm so I'm so starving”

**Natural**

“I'm starving.”

“Starving” already carries strong intensity, so **so** is usually unnecessary here.

#### 6. “a very detailed and helpful advice” → “really detailed and helpful advice”

**Original**

> “That's a very detailed and helpful advice”

**Natural**

“That's really detailed and helpful advice.”

**Advice** is uncountable, so normally do not use **a/an** before it.

#### 7. “extra toasty bun” → “extra-toasted bun”

**Original**

> “Extra toasty bun, please”

**Natural**

“Extra-toasted bun, please.”

“Toasty” describes something pleasantly warm/crisp; when requesting the preparation level, **extra toasted** is clearer.

## Replay

### Replay 1 — Make the point before explaining it

**Transcript**

> “I saw a video uh I saw a reels on I saw a video on reels uh the people is talking about how to pronounce correctly...”

↓

**Problem**

The idea was interesting, but several restarts delayed the point. The listener had to reconstruct the sentence while you were still choosing its structure.

↓

**Better Response**

“I saw a Reel about how Texans pronounce ‘Whataburger.’ Apparently locals say it a little differently.”

↓

**Why It Works**

The first sentence immediately establishes **what you saw + what it was about**. The second sentence adds the interesting detail. This structure works for almost any casual story.

---

### Replay 2 — Keep a personal story compact

**Transcript**

> “me and my and my girlfriend have watched this film before ten years ago. And it's my first, it's our first date. So it's quite a uh a meaningful to us.”

↓

**Problem**

The strongest detail—this was your first-date movie—was buried inside corrections and competing time expressions.

↓

**Better Response**

“My girlfriend and I saw it on our first date ten years ago, so seeing it again for the anniversary is pretty special for us.”

↓

**Why It Works**

One sentence gives the history, emotional meaning, and reason for the weekend plan. It gives the other person several natural things to respond to without becoming a monologue.

---

### Replay 3 — Transition instead of jumping

**Transcript**

> “what what do you think About Texas, Houston, Texas”

↓

**Problem**

The question was valid, but the switch from the movie discussion to Houston was abrupt.

↓

**Better Response**

“Speaking of living here, what do you think of Houston?”

↓

**Why It Works**

“Speaking of...” tells the listener that you are deliberately shifting to a related subject. The transition feels conversational rather than like moving to the next prepared question.
