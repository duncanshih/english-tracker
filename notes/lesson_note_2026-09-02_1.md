# Communication Review

### Conversation Timeline

Weather → interview tomorrow → preparation plans → request to switch
into mock interview → greeting practice → self-introduction →
audit-to-finance motivation → experience/role fit → request for feedback
→ concise answer model.

### 1. The main issue was answer architecture, not lack of content

The clearest example is the response beginning:

> "Um. For first question Uh I just- I just like a I have a- to observe
> the financial strategy financial decision made by clients..."

You had several relevant ideas: observing client decisions, wanting to
move closer to decision-making, a long-term CFO direction, audit
discipline, valuation, equity research, modeling, and company research.
The problem was that the listener had to wait for the structure to
emerge.

**Do differently:** give the map before the details. For a two-part
question, use: "There are two reasons. First... Second..." For
experience-fit questions, use: **Audit foundation → finance expansion →
relevance to this role.**

### 2. You sometimes continued adding evidence after the answer was already complete

After explaining that valuation and equity research sharpened modeling
and analytical skills, you continued through 10-Ks, 10-Qs, investor
presentations, products, clients, suppliers, margins, profitability,
upstream/downstream, supply chain, and investment theses.

That detail is useful when an interviewer asks specifically about your
research process, but here it diluted the answer to "how have those
experiences prepared you for this role?"

**Do differently:** stop once you have proved the point. A strong
interview answer can often end after three layers: **claim → one example
→ relevance.**

### 3. The opening interaction was workable, but some lines sounded scripted rather than conversational

You said:

> "Yeah, I agree. Yeah, thank- thank you for giving me this opportunity
> to interview. I really excited to know more about company and
> introduce myself"

The interviewer had only said they were glad to connect, so "I agree" is
not the most natural response. The rest also tries to accomplish too
much before the interviewer has asked the first question.

A cleaner handoff is: **"Likewise. Thanks for taking the time to meet
with me today."** Then let the interviewer lead.

### 4. You successfully used the conversation partner to repair the practice

When you felt the long answer had become difficult to control, you
stopped and explicitly asked:

> "for this question, do you have a any feedback for me..."

That was an effective training decision because it identified the exact
answer that needed restructuring instead of continuing the mock
interview. The useful lesson is not to interrupt a real interviewer this
way, but to keep using this behavior during practice: isolate the
answer, tighten it, then replay it.

# Grammar, Usage & Pronunciation

## Grammar

### Pattern 1: Adjective after "be"

**Original:** "I really excited to know more about company"

**Corrected:** "I'm really excited to learn more about the company."

Use **be + adjective**: "I am excited," not "I excited."

Drills: - "I'm excited about the interview tomorrow." - "I'm interested
in learning more about the role."

### Pattern 2: Singular/plural agreement with "experience"

**Original:** "Those two experience sharpen my modeling skills."

**Corrected:** "Those two experiences sharpened my modeling skills."

Use plural **experiences** after "two," and past tense for completed
internships.

Drills: - "Those three years gave me a strong accounting foundation." -
"My two internships strengthened my analytical skills."

### Pattern 3: Article with singular countable nouns

**Original:** "I got a interview tomorrow"

**Corrected:** "I have an interview tomorrow."

Use **an** before a vowel sound; "have an interview" is also the more
natural collocation here.

Drills: - "I have an interview tomorrow afternoon." - "I have an
opportunity to speak with the hiring manager."

## Usage

### "good fit to the company"

Natural version: **"a good fit for the company"** or **"a good fit for
the role."**

"Fit for" is the standard collocation when discussing compatibility with
a position or organization.

### "rigour in number"

Natural version: **"be rigorous with numbers"** or **"be very precise
with numbers."**

For interviews, "attention to detail" and "accuracy" are even more
natural when describing audit discipline.

### "focusing skill"

Natural version: **"analytical skills," "research skills,"** or
**"ability to focus,"** depending on the intended meaning.

In this answer, the transcript suggests you meant the skills developed
through valuation and equity research, so **"analytical and financial
modeling skills"** communicates the point more clearly.

# Conversation Toolkit

### 1. Weather before an interview

Useful expansion: - "It's a little cloudy today, so it feels much better
than last week." - "Last week was pretty brutal, so I'm enjoying the
cooler weather."

Reusable partner line from the conversation:

> "Yeah, it was brutal last week."

Use this pattern to react naturally to shared experiences: "Yeah,
traffic was brutal this morning," or "Yeah, the heat was brutal last
week."

### 2. Talking about interview preparation

Useful lines: - "I'm reviewing my key stories and matching them to the
job description." - "I'm trying not to memorize everything word for
word. I just want the key points to be clear." - "I'm doing one last
round of practice tonight."

Reusable partner line:

> "What's the big thing you're working on tonight?"

This is a useful casual way to ask what someone is focusing on before an
event or deadline.

### 3. Interview greeting

Useful sequence: - "Nice to meet you, Mr. Lai. Do you mind if I call you
Walter?" - "Great, thanks, Walter." - "Likewise. Thanks for taking the
time to meet with me today."

Avoid adding a mini self-introduction before the interviewer asks for
it.

### 4. Handling a two-part question

Useful signposts: - "There are two parts to that." - "On the first
point..." - "As for how my experience prepared me..." - "I'd highlight
two things."

These phrases buy you organizational space without sounding like filler.

### 5. Reusable conversation-partner line

> "I can see how those experiences fit together."

An interviewer or coworker can use this to acknowledge a story and
transition to a follow-up. A simpler reusable version is: **"I can see
how those pieces fit together."**
