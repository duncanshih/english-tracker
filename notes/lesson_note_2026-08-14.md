## Grammar, Usage & Pronunciation

### Grammar

#### 1. Past participles after “have”

**Original**

> “Yeah, I been to…”

> “I haven't eat ice cream outside”

**Corrected**

> “Yeah, I’ve been to…”

> “I haven’t eaten ice cream out.”

**Rule:** After **have/has/haven’t/hasn’t**, use the past participle: **been, eaten, seen, done**.

**Drill examples**

- “I’ve been to a few steakhouses in Chicago.”
- “I haven’t eaten out much lately because I still have ice cream at home.”

#### 2. Countable nouns and plurals

**Original**

> “I don't take bus”

> “I order ten cups of ice cream”

**Corrected**

> “I don’t take the bus.”

> “I ordered ten pints/containers of ice cream.”

**Rule:** Use the appropriate article/plural for countable nouns, and keep past events in the past tense.

**Drill examples**

- “I usually take the bus when I’m downtown.”
- “I ordered a bunch of different flavors on Prime Day.”

#### 3. “It takes me…” versus total driving time

**Original**

> “It's about two hour drive”

**Corrected**

> “I spend about two hours driving every day.”

or

> “It adds up to about two hours of driving a day.”

**Rule:** Use **spend + time + -ing** when describing the total amount of time you devote to an activity.

**Drill examples**

- “I spend almost an hour commuting each way.”
- “It adds up to a lot of time in the car.”

### Usage

#### 1. “Catch up errands”

**Original**

> “catch catch up some errands”

**Natural version**

> “catch up on some errands”

or simply:

> “run some errands”

**Why:** **Catch up on** means deal with things that have accumulated; **run errands** is the standard phrase for routine tasks such as shopping or picking things up.

#### 2. “Read books for leisure”

**Original**

> “Do you read books for for leisure”

**Natural version**

> “Do you ever read for fun?”

Alternatives:

> “Do you read much in your free time?”

> “Do you ever read just to relax?”

**Why:** “For leisure” is correct but more formal than ordinary office small talk.

#### 3. “Big amount”

**Original**

> “Is that a big amount?”

**Natural version**

> “Do you think that’s a lot for dinner?”

or

> “Is $300 crazy for dinner?”

**Why:** Native speakers usually evaluate a price with **a lot**, **expensive**, or **crazy**, rather than “a big amount.”

#### 4. “Think to quit”

**Original**

> “Don't don't you think to quit”

**Natural version**

> “Wouldn’t you think about quitting?”

or

> “Would that make you consider quitting?”

**Why:** English uses **think about + -ing** or **consider + -ing**, not “think to quit.”

#### 5. “Open some topic”

**Original**

> “Can you open some topic”

**Natural version**

> “Can you pick a topic?”

or

> “What else should we talk about?”

**Why:** English speakers **bring up**, **start**, or **pick** a topic; they do not normally “open a topic” in casual conversation.

#### 6. “I am a little bit drowsy”

This sentence is natural. In a driving context, an even more immediate phrase is:

> “I’m getting a little drowsy.”

That emphasizes the state developing while you drive.

### 5. Food and flavor

**Useful description pattern**

> “It starts off ___, then you get a hint of ___.”

> “It’s ___ and slightly ___, with a ___ note.”

> “The texture is tender and slightly crumbly.”

**Today’s distinctions**

- **a hint of X** = a small/subtle amount of a flavor
- **an X note** = a recognizable flavor characteristic within the overall taste
- **savory** = not primarily sweet; rich/salty/umami-like
- **crumbly** = breaks into small soft pieces easily

**Reusable partner line from today**

> “A ‘hint’ usually means just a tiny amount. It's barely there.”

### 6. Closing naturally

Your actual closing was:

> “Thank you Alright. It was a nice talking with you Bye-bye”

A more natural office version:

> “All right, it was nice talking with you. Have a good one.”

or, before an interview:

> “Well, I should probably get ready. Nice talking with you.”

This avoids needing the other person to introduce another topic once the conversation has naturally run its course.

### Replay 1 — Organize the thought before speaking

**Transcript**

### Replay 2 — Build a workplace hypothetical cleanly

**Transcript**

> “what if uh when this five minutes left and you are going to get off and then you receive a email Your supervisor told me to do something In today”

↓

**Problem**

The scenario contains several useful details, but they appear before the grammatical structure is established.

↓

**Better Response**

> “What would you do if your manager sent you an urgent request five minutes before you were supposed to leave?”

↓

**Why It Works**

One sentence contains the actor, timing, urgency, and decision point. It sounds natural and is easy to answer.

---

### Replay 3 — Do not rely on the partner to rescue a fading conversation

**Transcript**

> “Oh yeah I don't have any question”

> “So Uh. Can you open some topic”

↓

**Problem**

The conversation has reached a natural lull. Asking the other person to generate a topic exposes that you have run out of material; in real office small talk, the conversation might simply end there.

↓

**Better Response**

Option A — continue:

> “Anyway, any plans for the weekend?”

Option B — close:

> “Well, I should probably get ready. Nice talking with you.”

↓

**Why It Works**

You make a deliberate conversational choice: continue with a familiar small-talk bridge or exit cleanly. You do not depend on the other person to rescue the interaction.
