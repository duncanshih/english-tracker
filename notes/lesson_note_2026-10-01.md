# Communication Review

## Conversation Timeline

Opening → Houston weather → commute → grocery shopping/cooking → recruiter questions → audit-to-accounting transition → audit experience → sales-tax gap → strengths/weaknesses → self-introduction.

The session therefore contained two different communication environments. The early section tested ordinary small talk; the later and much longer section tested interview-answer construction.

### 1. The main problem was answer architecture, especially in the self-introduction

The clearest example was:

> “And I realized that this is just from to audit this number. I would like to uh work close- work- more close- be work closely with the number and know what jobs the- decision…”

The underlying idea is useful: audit exposed you to businesses, but you now want to move from reviewing numbers to owning the accounting process and working closer to operations. The difficulty arose because the sentence attempted to explain the realization, career transition, and desired future role simultaneously.

**Do differently:** use a three-block structure:

1. **Past:** what you did.
2. **Transition:** what you learned/wanted next.
3. **Fit:** why this role is the logical next step.

Finish each block before moving on.

### 2. Concrete details made the small talk easier to continue

Your grocery/cooking answer gave the partner material to use:

> “I like to go buying grocery for um for unwinding…”

and then:

> “I- I love uh cooking the the ingredients I buy”

That led naturally to:

> “What's your, like, go-to dish to make right now?”

This is a useful small-talk pattern. A specific activity—grocery shopping, cooking, a particular dish—creates an obvious follow-up path. Compare that with:

> “Pretty smooth. Yeah”

That answer was complete, but it gave the partner very little new material. In a real conversation, that can be perfectly acceptable if you want the topic to end. If you want it to continue, add one detail: “Pretty smooth, actually. I left a little early, so I missed most of the traffic.”

### 3. You found a strong professional bridge between audit and accounting

A central line developed during the session was:

> “I'm looking to move from reviewing the accounting process to owning the accounting process.”

This is effective because it does not apologize for lacking in-house accounting experience. It explains the career move as a progression. The same structure also worked for the sales-tax gap: acknowledge what you have not done, identify relevant experience, then explain why the underlying skills transfer.

This framing should be retained, but it should be delivered in your own words rather than memorized as a long script.

### 4. Repeated requests for exact repetition suggest the practice became script-dependent

You repeatedly asked:

> “can you repeat it again”

and:

> “Sorry, slower, please”

Repetition helped isolate useful language, but the session increasingly became reproduction of prepared sentences. For interview performance, the risk is that losing one memorized phrase can disrupt the rest of the answer.

**Do differently:** memorize only the structure and anchor phrases. For example:

**Audit background → exposure → transferable skills → why in-house now.**

Then practice giving three slightly different versions. That trains retrieval rather than recitation.

### 5. The weakness answer had the right behavioral logic but needed compression

Your original idea contained all the necessary pieces:

> “I'm still improving is uh speaking up earlier…”

> “before every meeting, I usually prepare like three to five points…”

That already has the right structure: genuine weakness → consequence → specific action. The main improvement is to remove the extended explanation about whether your idea is valuable and move quickly to what you now do differently.

A compact answer will be easier to retrieve during an interview.

# Grammar, Usage & Pronunciation

## Grammar

### Pattern 1: singular/plural after numbers and “one of”

**Original**

> “seven company”

**Corrected**

“seven companies”

Rule: Use a plural count noun after a number greater than one.

Drills:
- “I led engagements for seven companies.”
- “I prepared three to five points before each meeting.”

### Pattern 2: past tense for completed experience

**Original**

> “in my final year, I lead…”

**Corrected**

“In my final year, I led…”

Rule: Use the simple past for completed responsibilities in a previous job.

Drills:
- “I led audit engagements in my final year.”
- “I worked closely with client accounting teams.”

### Pattern 3: verb form after “be”

**Original**

> “the team I will be work with”

**Corrected**

“the team I’ll be working with”

Rule: After “will be,” use the -ing form when describing an ongoing future working relationship.

Drills:
- “Who will I be working with?”
- “What systems will I be using?”

## Usage

### “go buying grocery”

**Original**

> “I like to go buying grocery”

**Natural**

“I like to go grocery shopping.”

“Grocery shopping” is the standard expression for the activity.

Nearby alternatives:
- “I like going to the grocery store.”
- “I find grocery shopping relaxing.”

### “during work in here”

**Original**

> “what do you enjoy the most during work in here”

**Natural**

“What do you enjoy most about working here?”

The natural pattern is **enjoy + most + about + gerund** in this context.

### “I’m fear of speaking up”

**Original**

> “I- I'm fear of speaking up early”

**Natural**

“I’m afraid to speak up early” / “I sometimes hesitate to speak up early.”

For an interview, “I sometimes hesitate to…” is especially useful because it describes the behavior without sounding overly dramatic.

### “I didn’t well prepare”

**Original**

> “I didn't well prepare”

**Natural**

“I didn’t prepare well.” / “I wasn’t well prepared.”

Adverbs such as “well” normally follow the verb in this construction, or “well prepared” can function as an adjective phrase after “be.”

# Conversation Toolkit

## 1. Houston weather

Useful expansion:
- “It’s definitely getting better compared with last month.”
- “Last month was brutal, but lately it’s been a little more comfortable.”

Useful return question:
- “Do you usually like Houston weather this time of year?”

Reusable partner line:

> “At least it's easing up a little.”

Use this when agreeing that an unpleasant situation—heat, traffic, workload—is gradually improving.

## 2. Commute

Useful answer:
- “It took me about thirty minutes today, and traffic was actually pretty smooth.”

Useful follow-up:
- “Is the traffic usually worse around here in the morning?”

Reusable partner line:

> “That's not too bad by Houston standards.”

Useful as a light reaction whenever someone mentions a Houston commute that was manageable.

## 3. Unwinding and cooking

Useful answer:
- “I actually like grocery shopping when I want to unwind. Then I usually cook something when I get home.”

Useful expansion:
- “Lately I’ve been making shakshuka quite a bit.”

Reusable partner line:

> “What's your, like, go-to dish to make right now?”

Use this pattern to continue any food or hobby topic:
- “What’s your go-to place for lunch?”
- “What’s your go-to coffee order?”

## 4. Asking the recruiter questions

Retain these:

- “What are the biggest priorities for this role in the first three to six months?”
- “What does success look like in this position?”
- “Could you tell me a bit about the team I’d be working with?”
- “What do you enjoy most about working here?”
- “What are the next steps in the process?”

Do not memorize all five as a fixed sequence. Choose two or three based on what the interviewer has already covered.

## 5. Bridging an experience gap

Core structure:

**Acknowledge → Transfer → Motivation**

Example:

“I haven’t directly owned sales-tax filings yet, so that would be a new area for me. But I do have experience with tax compliance and detailed financial reconciliations, so the core skills are transferable. I’d be comfortable learning the company’s specific filing process.”

Reusable partner concept from the session:

> “I'm moving from reviewing the accounting process to owning the accounting process.”

This is useful whenever you need to explain why audit experience is relevant to an in-house accounting role.

# Replay

## Replay 1 — Keep small talk alive with one concrete detail

**Transcript**

> “Pretty smooth. Yeah”

↓

**Problem**

The answer completed the question but supplied no new conversational material. If the other person does not introduce another topic, the exchange can stall.

↓

**Better Response**

“Pretty smooth, actually. It took about thirty minutes, so I got lucky with traffic today.”

**Why It Works**

It remains short but gives the other person two hooks: commute time and unusually light traffic.

## Replay 2 — Explain an experience gap without becoming defensive

**Transcript**

> “I am from audit background I don't have a general accounting experience like hands-on general accounting experience…”

↓

**Problem**

The answer begins by emphasizing what is missing and then searches for the next idea. That makes it harder to pivot toward your relevant experience.

↓

**Better Response**

“My background is primarily in audit, so I haven’t owned the full accounting cycle in-house yet. But I’ve worked closely with accounting teams and reviewed those processes for several years. Now I want to move from reviewing the process to owning it.”

↓

**Why It Works**

It acknowledges the gap once, then spends most of the answer on relevant experience and career direction.

---

## Replay 3 — Build the self-introduction in three blocks

**Transcript**

> “And I realized that this is just from to audit this number. I would like to uh work close- work- more close- be work closely with the number…”

↓

**Problem**

Several ideas are being constructed simultaneously, so the career-transition point becomes difficult to complete.

↓

**Better Response**

“Those three years gave me strong exposure to financial reporting and how different businesses operate. But I realized I wanted to move beyond reviewing the numbers and work closer to the accounting process itself. That’s what led me to graduate study and why I’m now interested in an in-house accounting role.”

↓

**Why It Works**

Each sentence has one job: experience → realization → next step. If one sentence changes during the interview, the overall structure remains intact.
