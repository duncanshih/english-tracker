# Grammar, Usage & Pronunciation

## Grammar

### 1. Recent completed action: "just finished"

**Original**

> "Just- just finish my workout"

**Corrected**

"I just finished my workout."

**Rule:** Use the past tense for a completed action that happened just
before now.

**Drill** - "I just finished my coffee." - "I just got off the
treadmill."

### 2. Articles with countable nouns

**Original**

> "do you have table for three"

**Corrected**

"Do you have **a** table for three?"

**Rule:** A singular countable noun normally needs an article or another
determiner.

**Drill** - "Could I get **an** iced latte?" - "I'd like to make **an**
appointment."

### 3. "A party of three"

**Original**

> "is there a wait for party of three"

**Corrected**

"Is there a wait for **a** party of three?"

**Rule:** Use "a" before the singular countable phrase "party of three."

**Drill** - "We have a party of two." - "Do you have a table for a party
of four?"

### 4. Plural after "these"

**Original**

> "Could you shuck these oyster, please"

**Corrected**

"Could you shuck these **oysters**, please?"

**Rule:** "These" requires a plural countable noun.

**Drill** - "Could you fillet these fish, please?" - "Could you put
these items in a bag?"

## Usage

### 1. "Traffic is busy"

**Original**

> "the traffic is a little bit busy today"

**Natural**

"Traffic is a little heavy today." / "Traffic's worse than usual today."

"Heavy" is the standard collocation for traffic volume; "busy" is
understandable but less idiomatic here.

### 2. "Fastest road"

**Original**

> "I just take the fastest road"

**Natural**

"I just take the fastest route."

"Route" refers to the overall path chosen to reach a destination, which
fits GPS/navigation better than "road."

### 3. "Perfect for commute"

**Original**

> "it's perfect for commute"

**Natural**

"It's perfect for **the commute**." / "It's perfect for commuting."

Both versions sound natural when describing something you listen to
while traveling to or from work.

### 4. "Doesn't fit my need"

**Original**

> "this air fryer doesn't fit my need"

**Natural**

"This air fryer doesn't really meet my needs."\
Nearby alternative: "It just doesn't work for what I need."

"Meet my needs" is the standard expression for whether a product suits
your requirements.

### 5. "Reservation" vs. "appointment"

For a haircut, **appointment** is the normal word:

"I'd like to make a haircut appointment."

For a restaurant, **reservation** is the normal word:

"I'd like to make a reservation for three."

# Conversation Toolkit

### 1. Commute and traffic

Useful expansion: - "Traffic's a little worse than usual today." - "I
usually just take whatever route the GPS says is fastest."

Useful return question: - "How was traffic for you?" - "What do you
usually listen to in the car?"

Reusable partner line from the session:

> "Usually just throw on a podcast or music."

Use **throw on** casually when you mean quickly putting on music, a
podcast, a show, or even clothing.

### 2. Restaurant phone call

Opening: - "Hi, do you have a table for three available right now?"

If there is no immediate table: - "What's the wait for three?" - "How
long is the wait for a party of three?"

If you're leaving now: - "Great, we'll head over."

If you're almost there: - "We'll be right there."

Reusable partner line:

> "Great, we'll head over."

This is useful whenever you have confirmed availability and are now
leaving for the location.

### 3. Coffee order

Compact order: - "Could I get a large iced matcha latte with whole milk,
half sweet, light ice, please?"

Reusable partner line:

> "What can I get started for you?"

Expect this as a normal U.S. coffee-shop version of "What would you
like?"

### 4. Seafood counter

-   "Could you scale and gut it, please?"
-   "Could you remove the head?"
-   "Could you fillet it, please?"
-   "Could you cut it into steaks?"
-   "Could you shuck these oysters, please?"

The useful pattern is **Could you + service + please?** You can reuse it
without building a longer explanation.

### 5. Haircut appointment

Shortest useful version: - "Hi, I'd like to book a haircut with Jason."

If you need to negotiate timing: - "Do you have anything Friday
afternoon?" - "Friday afternoon works for me."

Reusable partner line:

> "Do you have anything Friday afternoon?"

This pattern works for appointments generally: "Do you have anything
Monday morning / after 3 / this weekend?"

### 6. Clean exit

-   "Nice talking with you. I've gotta head out."
-   "I've gotta head out and pick up my girlfriend. See you."

The important behavior is to close once, clearly, instead of continuing
to explain after the goodbye has already begun.
