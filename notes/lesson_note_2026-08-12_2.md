# Communication Review

**Conversation timeline:** greeting/tone adjustment → restaurant seating → drinks → red-wine ordering → wine names → wine differences → tasting vocabulary → steak pairing → closing.

The strongest structural behavior was curiosity. Once the wine topic started, you repeatedly took information from the partner's previous answer and used it to generate the next question. For example, after hearing about body, tannins, and acidity, you asked, **“So what is body”**, and after learning about body, you moved to **“What kind of red wine … [is] perfect to pair with … steak.”** This created a connected topic chain rather than a sequence of unrelated questions.

The main issue was question construction. Several questions contained restarts before the listener could identify the core request: **“So So. Can you introduce- the difference between the amount, these three types of red wine”** and **“What's the What's the What kind of red wine uh can is. Perfect to pair with with steak.”** The intended meaning eventually became understandable, but the listener had to wait through multiple partial structures. A better strategy is to choose a simple frame first: **“What’s the difference between these three wines?”** or **“What red wine pairs well with steak?”**

There were also a few direct translations or word choices that could create misunderstanding. **“What kind of house bread, you have”** appears to mean *house red*, but “bread” changes the meaning completely. **“Can you introduce the difference…”** is understandable but not idiomatic for asking someone to compare products. “Can you explain the difference…?” or simply “What’s the difference…?” is more natural.

Your closing was clear—**“Alright, it was nice talking to you. It's time to say goodbye”**—but it sounds like commentary about ending a practice session rather than something a person would normally say to another person. In a real interaction, **“All right, it was nice talking to you. See you later.”** would complete the handoff naturally.

# Grammar, Usage & Pronunciation

## Grammar

### Question formation

**Original:** “Why you are so nasty”

**Corrected:** “Why are you being so nasty?” / “Why do you sound so nasty?”

In direct questions, the auxiliary verb normally comes before the subject.

Drills:
- “What drinks do you have?”
- “What red wine would you recommend?”

### “How do you…” questions

**Original:** “How to how to spell pinot Merlot”

**Corrected:** “How do you spell Merlot?”

When asking another person for a method or spelling, use “How do you…?”

Drills:
- “How do you pronounce Cabernet?”
- “How do you taste red wine properly?”

### Countable plural

**Original:** “these three red wine”

**Corrected:** “these three red wines”

When referring to several individual wines, use the plural “wines.”

Drills:
- “What’s the difference between these three wines?”
- “Which wines pair well with steak?”

## Usage

### “Know much about”

**Original:** “I don't know red wine very much”

**Natural:** “I don’t know much about red wine.”

This is the standard pattern for describing limited knowledge of a subject.

Nearby alternatives:
- “I’m not very familiar with red wine.”
- “I’m pretty new to wine.”

### “Explain the difference”

**Original:** “Can you introduce- the difference…”

**Natural:** “Can you explain the difference…?”

“Introduce” usually presents a person or topic; “explain” is used when asking for distinctions or reasons.

### “Pair with”

**Original:** “Perfect to pair with with steak”

**Natural:** “pairs well with steak”

For food and wine, “X pairs well with Y” is the common collocation.

### “Repeat that”

**Original:** “Can you repeat again”

**Natural:** “Can you repeat that?” / “Could you say that again?”

“Repeat” already contains the meaning of “again,” so using both is redundant.
