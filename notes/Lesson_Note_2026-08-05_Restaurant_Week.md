## 1. You successfully created connected topic transitions

You did not wait for the associate to introduce every subject. After discussing your Mexican restaurant plan, you returned the conversation with:

> “What about you? Do you have any thoughts for Restaurant Week?”

That was the clearest successful handoff in the session. It invited the other person to contribute and directly produced the next topic, Italian food. You then connected the associate’s Italian restaurant idea to your own experience:

> “Italian cuisine, I love Italian cuisine, because, you know, since I lived in St. Louis for two years,”

The sentence itself was unfinished, but the conversational decision was useful: you connected the partner’s topic to a personal experience rather than switching subjects randomly.

You also used:

> “speaking of Italian cuisine, do you know what's the difference between Italian cuisine and French cuisine?”

“Speaking of…” is an effective transition because it shows how the new question relates to the existing topic.

**What to keep doing:** Continue using a three-step pattern: react to the other person’s point, add one personal detail, and return a question.

# Grammar, Usage & Pronunciation

## Grammar

### 1. Relative clauses for things and people

**Original**

> “a Mexican restaurant, who is operated by a famous chef who is a Mexican”

**Corrected**

“a Mexican restaurant that is run by a well-known Mexican chef”

A restaurant is a thing, so use “that” or “which,” while “who” refers to a person.

**Drill examples**

- “We’re trying an Italian restaurant that opened downtown.”
- “The chef who runs it is originally from Italy.”

### 2. Access as a verb pattern

**Original**

> “you can easily access to seafood”

**Corrected**

“you can easily access seafood”  
or  
“you have easy access to seafood”

Use “access” directly as a verb, or use “access to” after the noun phrase “have access.”

**Drill examples**

- “Houston has easy access to fresh seafood.”
- “Restaurants here can access ingredients from many different regions.”

### 3. Plural agreement after “those types”

**Original**

> “those type of dish”

**Corrected**

“those types of dishes”

A plural demonstrative and plural head noun require a plural count noun after “of.”

**Drill examples**

- “Those types of restaurants usually require reservations.”
- “I like those kinds of set menus.”

## Usage

### 1. Describing traffic

**Original**

> “It's a little bit stuck.”

**Natural version**

“Traffic was a little backed up.”  
or  
“I got stuck in a little traffic.”

“Stuck” describes the person or car; “backed up” describes the traffic.

### 2. Describing participation in an event

**Original**

> “300 restaurants in Houston joining the restaurant week”

**Natural version**

“around 300 Houston restaurants are participating in Restaurant Week”

“Participate in” is the standard phrase for restaurants taking part in an organized event.

Nearby alternatives:

- “Around 300 restaurants are taking part.”
- “There are about 300 participating restaurants.”

### 3. Describing restaurant value

**Original**

> “you can enjoy this restaurant with a good price”

**Natural version**

“you can try the restaurant at a good price”  
or  
“you can get a good deal at the restaurant”

English normally uses “at a good price,” not “with a good price.”

### 4. Referring to a country

**Original**

> “However, in French”

**Natural version**

“However, in France”  
or  
“When it comes to French cuisine”

“French” is the adjective or language; “France” is the country.

### 5. Expressing opening timing

**Original**

> “I don't know when, when is the specific time, but I know it's gonna open.”

**Natural version**

“I’m not sure exactly when it’s opening, but I heard it’s coming soon.”

This version removes the repeated indirect-question structure and uses the natural expression “coming soon.”

- “My commute was slower than usual, but it wasn’t terrible.”

- “Do you usually take the freeway?”

Use this when the other person has already mentioned traffic or driving.

- “Have you looked at any of the menus?”
- “Is there anywhere you’ve been wanting to try?”

> “Any spot on your list you’re thinking of checking out?”

Use this after someone mentions a food event, weekend dining plan, or list of restaurants.

**Useful handoff**

**Reusable learner line**

## 4. Italian food and St. Louis

**Useful transition**

- “That reminds me of a neighborhood in St. Louis called The Hill.”
- “When I lived in St. Louis, I used to go to an Italian-American neighborhood called The Hill.”

**Useful expansion**

- “It’s one of the best places there for authentic Italian food.”

**Reusable conversation-partner line**

> “I’m eyeing this little Italian place I’ve heard about.”

Use “I’m eyeing…” when you are considering a restaurant, destination, or purchase but have not decided yet.

## 5. Comparing two cuisines

**Useful structure**

- “Do you usually go for Italian or French food?”

**Useful exit when the topic becomes too analytical**

- “That’s just my impression, though.”
- “Either way, I’d happily eat both.”
